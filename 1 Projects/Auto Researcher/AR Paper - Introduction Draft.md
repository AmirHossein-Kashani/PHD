**Scientific agents and autonomous researchers**
LLM-based systems for science have moved quickly from assistants to agents. A recent survey organizes them into three levels of autonomy, Tool, Analyst and Scientist, according to how much of the research lifecycle the model owns and how much the human retains (Zheng et al., 2025). At the Tool level the model performs a single explicitly defined task, such as literature search, code completion or data cleaning, and the human allocates the work. At the Analyst level the model carries out a multi-step analysis of given data with limited supervision, for instance a data-analysis agent that writes and runs code until it answers a question. At the Scientist level the model runs the discovery cycle itself: it proposes hypotheses, designs and executes experiments, interprets the results and revises its plan, with humans setting the objective and judging the output. Systems at this level now exist for several domains: The AI Scientist and its successor for machine-learning research (Lu et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2025), the AI co-scientist for biomedical hypothesis generation (Gottweis et al., 2025), AI-Researcher for idea-to-paper pipelines (Tang et al., 2025), Kosmos for data-driven discovery over long runs (Mitchener et al., 2025), and Coscientist for autonomous chemistry with laboratory hardware (Boiko et al., 2023). [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13259)
**Our level.** Our system is a Scientist-level agent with a verifiable objective. It receives a task, a dataset and an automatic evaluator, and runs the full loop unattended for days: it generates ideas, implements them, evaluates them locally, decides what to keep, and submits results, while humans only define the task and the rules and choose the final submissions. This is narrower than the open-ended discovery of Kosmos or the paper-writing goal of The AI Scientist-v2, and deliberately so: an external score makes it possible to measure whether a change to the research machinery changed the outcome, which is the question this proposal asks.
**Limitations of current autonomous researchers**
*Long horizon means long context.* A research campaign is a long sequence of dependent actions, and the depth an agent can reach is bounded by how long it stays coherent. Even the longest-running system to date notes that all such agents remain limited in the number of actions they can take before losing coherence, which limits the depth of their findings (Mitchener et al., 2025). The cause is partly the model: long-context benchmarks that remove literal cues show performance degrading sharply as context grows, with 11 of 13 models falling below half of their short-context accuracy at 32K tokens (Modarressi et al., 2025), consistent with earlier findings that models lose information placed in the middle of long inputs (Liu et al., 2024). The cost is also practical: in our pilot, about 77% of all model usage was agents re-reading their own context. Two sub-problems follow. [papers](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2511.02824)[mlanthology](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2025/modarressi2025icml-nolima)
*Long-term planning.* Research requires plans that survive many cycles and are revised on evidence. On planning benchmarks drawn from the automated-planning community, LLM performance falls well short even for the strongest models (Valmeekam et al., 2023), and progress has been slow across model generations (Valmeekam et al., 2024), which has led to the position that LLMs should act as candidate-plan generators inside a loop with external verifiers rather than as planners (Kambhampati et al., 2024). Current research agents reflect this: planning is either a fixed stage sequence or left to the model, and in our pilot the arm that left planning optional stopped early with budget unused, while the arm with enforced plan reviews produced the only revisions of blocked ideas. [neurips.cc +2](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2023/poster/73553)
*Memory management.* Because the full history cannot stay in context, every long-running agent compresses it. General agent memories do this with summaries and retrieval (Park et al., 2023; Packer et al., 2023) or with reflections on past failures (Shinn et al., 2023); research agents do it with a best node per stage, an advisor verdict, or a shared world-model summary. What all of these drop is the path: why the agent is where it is, what it committed to on the way, and what evidence would make it turn back. Without that path, an agent cannot revise a commitment or notice that it is repeating itself, which is where the exploration problems of the next section begin.

            The acceleration that LLM-driven search can deliver is already dramatic. Google’s AI co-scientist, given only pre-discovery public data, reproduced in two days the cf-PICI spread mechanism that an Imperial College team had spent roughly ten years establishing (Gottweis et al., 2025; Penadés et al., 2025). AlphaEvolve found a 48-multiplication algorithm for 4×4 complex matrices, the first improvement on Strassen’s 1969 result in 56 years, and cut kernel-optimization engineering from weeks of expert effort to days of automated experiments (Novikov et al., 2025). Even with no change to the underlying model, structuring inference as explicit tree search raised GPT-4’s success rate on Game of 24 from 4% to 74% (Yao et al., 2023). The bottleneck is shifting from whether a solution can be reached to how quickly, and how to allocate the search budget to get there.             **Creativity, exploration and exploitation**
            Creativity can be treated as a search problem. Problem solving is a search through a space of possible states (Newell & Simon, 1972), and creative output is the part of that search that reaches states which are both new and useful (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). Classic accounts describe the process as generating many variations and keeping the good ones (Campbell, 1960; Boden, 1998), so creative yield depends on how a fixed thinking budget is split between producing new candidates and refining existing ones. For LLMs this budget is measurable: coverage grows with the number of samples (Brown et al., 2024), and adaptive allocation of inference compute is several times more efficient than uniform spending (Snell et al., 2025). The split is the exploration–exploitation trade-off at the core of reinforcement learning (Sutton & Barto, 2018), where an agent must choose between the action it currently believes is best and an uncertain one that might be better, and where the standard failure is under-exploration, because refinement gives small reliable gains and exploration gives rare large ones. LLM agents inherit this bias: in simple bandit environments, GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Llama2 did not explore robustly, and only one configuration, GPT-4 with chain-of-thought and an externally summarized history, behaved acceptably (Krishnamurthy et al., 2024). Exploration therefore has to be built into the scaffold, as RL builds it into the policy, rather than expected from the model.
            The sharpest form of under-exploration is an agent defending an idea it already holds. In humans this appears as confirmation bias (Nickerson, 1998), design fixation on an initial example (Jansson & Smith, 1991) and escalation of commitment (Staw, 1976). In LLMs self-reflection makes it worse: once a model is confident in a solution it cannot produce new thoughts through further reflection, even when its initial stance is wrong (Liang et al., 2024), and models struggle to self-correct reasoning without external feedback, sometimes getting worse after trying (Huang et al., 2024). In our preliminary experiments with four research scaffolds on a shared task, we observed the same behaviour in some cases: an idea that had been blocked with an explicit reopening condition stayed blocked after the condition was met, agents continued to trust a local validation that had already misled them, and when a merge tool refused a change they bypassed it rather than revising the idea. Known remedies either add external pressure, such as multi-agent debate (Liang et al., 2024) or multiple working hypotheses (Chamberlin, 1890; Platt, 1964), or make exploration a budgeted quantity, as in novelty search and quality-diversity archives (Lehman & Stanley, 2011; Mouret & Clune, 2015). The systems we tested offered such steps only as optional behaviour, and optional behaviour was rarely used. Our design therefore treats them as part of the loop: each commitment is recorded together with the evidence that would overturn it, that evidence is checked whenever new results arrive, and part of every cycle is reserved for ideas far from the current path.

    **Autonomous research systems and their limits**
    Three recent systems are the closest to our setting. The AI Scientist-v2 (Yamada et al., 2025) removes human-written code templates and replaces the linear pipeline of its predecessor with an agentic tree search managed by an experiment-manager agent; nodes are code states with results, and the best-performing nodes of one stage seed the next. Research is forced through four stages (preliminary investigation, hyperparameter tuning, research agenda, ablations), with ideation deliberately decoupled from implementation. Its authors note that the system still struggles to generate novel, high-impact hypotheses and to justify design decisions, and because each stage keeps only its best node, the alternatives explored earlier are discarded rather than revisited. AI-Researcher (Tang et al., 2025) runs a complete pipeline from literature review and hypothesis generation to implementation and manuscript, and introduces Scientist-Bench, a set of state-of-the-art AI papers used as targets. Its strongest idea is to start from a set of input papers, but idea refinement is thin: several ideas are generated, only the best is implemented, and the only revision step is an advisor deciding whether to continue. The benchmark also relies solely on LLM judges, and its target papers are likely in the models’ training data. Kosmos (Mitchener et al., 2025) runs for up to 12 hours over about 200 agent rollouts, using a structured world model to share information between data-analysis and literature-search agents, executing on average 42,000 lines of code and reading 1,500 papers per run. Every statement in its reports is cited to code or primary literature, and independent scientists judged 79.4% of its statements accurate. Its contribution is context separation: many short agents write into one shared summary. What the summary does not keep is the path by which any conclusion was reached, so a failed line of work survives only as its outcome, not as the sequence of decisions that produced it. The open-source idea-tree system we build on (Arbor) sits between these: it keeps a tree of ideas with pending, done, merged and pruned states, but, as our pilot showed, a pruned idea is rarely reconsidered. [close this message +6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08066v1)
    Taken together, these systems address the long-horizon problem by compressing history (a best node, an advisor verdict, a world-model summary) and address exploration by generating several candidates once, early. Neither keeps the agent’s own reasoning path as a first-class object, which is what revising a commitment, detecting fixation, and budgeting exploration all require.
    **Our contributions**
    Our design keeps the same model and the same research loop as the baselines and changes the research state the agent reasons over.
    *Local knowledge: the path to the root.* Alongside a shared knowledge base of important results and of approaches that succeeded or failed, which Kosmos-style summaries already provide, each agent carries a local record of its current path: the chain of actions and results from the idea it is now working on back to the root of its reasoning, with each step holding the decision taken, the evidence it rested on, and the condition that would overturn it. This addresses the long-horizon limitation directly, because the agent loads only its path rather than the whole history, so context grows with depth rather than with time. It differs from the AI Scientist-v2 tree, where nodes hold code and scores but not the reasons for choosing them, and from the Kosmos world model, which keeps conclusions but not paths. It also addresses fixation: a commitment whose overturning condition is now met is flagged on every cycle rather than left for the agent to notice.
    *Trajectory of thinking.* The local paths of all agents form an append-only trajectory of the run: plan, implementation, result, revision. Baselines log steps for audit, as Kosmos does with citations; we use the trajectory as a control signal. It tells the loop when an agent has spent several cycles on one branch with no gain, when two agents are converging on the same idea, and how far the current candidates sit from everything already tried, which is what the exploration budget in each cycle is spent against.
    *Failure refinement.* When an idea fails, the path records why and what would have to change, so the idea can be refined rather than discarded. Our current rule for this is preliminary and is the component we expect to revise most.
